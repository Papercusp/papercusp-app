Design · revised after review · 2026-08-30

The Missing Hop

The Email and Calendar apps are correctly generalized — neither touches Google. The platform normalizes Gmail and Calendar into canonical types and holds 711 real rows. Nothing carries those rows the last step into the apps.

Your four answers landed. Two of them changed the design rather than annotating it, and your question about the rules engine turned out to make one decision better — it deletes a problem the first draft only routed around.

What the review changed

Four answers, and what each one moved

Freshness — “The data should be instantly fresh.”

D-001 is revised, not annotated. The first draft chose a scheduled routine and explicitly accepted “latency is one interval, not real-time,” deferring push to “later, as an optimization.” Instantly-fresh removes that trade, so the design is now both paths, each doing the job it is actually good at: push delivers on the event; the cursor-driven batch backfills and reconciles. Push alone could never notice it had dropped something.

Scope — “Multi-tenant now.”

The owner mapping (P-003) becomes required from day one and its risk goes to primary. The apps scope every store read by ownerId via runtime.serviceFor(ownerId), so a wrong mapping doesn’t fail loudly — it delivers one person’s mail into another person’s app.

Backfill — “Backfill everything.”

The whole corpus, not a recent window. With one caveat that the measurement itself forced: the corpus is live. The newest message landed minutes before this was written, so backfill is sized as “everything before the cursor,” never as a frozen row count — and no acceptance evidence may cite a hardcoded total.

Judgment — “What if we set up a rule in our rules system?”

That rule already exists, which makes your framing better than my design. Binding b7976842 is exactly it. So Email’s trigger endpoint firing judgment isn’t the judgment path — it’s a second, duplicate one living inside the app. D-003 routed around that conflation; the answer is to delete it. Full reasoning two sections down.

Your architectural question

Did we build the trigger system on the rules engine?

Substantially yes — verified in code, not from memory. @papercusp/rules is a real, tested engine: engine, matcher, operators, path, schema, compile, validate, each with its own test file. The binding engine evaluates every binding through it — binding-engine.ts:508 calls payloadMatchesFilter(binding.eventFilter, event.payload), and that module’s own header states it “REUSES @papercusp/rules’ mingo-backed matches operator — the SAME leaf evaluator the ECA reaction engine’s when uses… We do NOT hand-roll a second regex/glob matcher.” The same engine backs events:await, events:watch, events:emit, the events graph, and the coord inbox bus.

And your proposed rule is not hypothetical. Binding b7976842 is armed on a connected source, and its filter is a genuine rules condition, not a string match:

Armed

b7976842 — the rule you described

ext:gmail:message.received → create work item email-draft-proposal, when all: [direction=inbound, threadId exists, from exists, subject exists, messageId exists].

The layering is right: rules decide whether; the action layer owns what. A rules engine should not own what.

The real limit

Two actions, one of them hardcoded

actionType() resolves exactly two: launch-plan and create-work-item. And directEmailWorkItem throws external_trigger_unsupported_direct_target unless the target is precisely Email’s draft-proposal kind.

So a rule can decide whether generically but can only do two things, one of which knows one app by name. That is why content delivery is a sink, not a rule.

“Should we have?”

The parts that exist are right — don’t redo them. The one real gap: rules are adopted for conditions, but there is no single surface where you can see every rule at once. Trigger bindings, event awaits, and ECA reactions each carry their own. That’s a genuine opportunity, and a separate concern I’m deliberately not smuggling into this plan.

Corrections I owe

Two things in the first draft were wrong

D-001’s third reason for rejecting a sink was invalid

I argued that a new sink kind “would be closer to a third” recurring-work mechanism, which repo convention forbids. That conflates two unrelated things. The two-mechanism rule governs recurring work. A delivery sink is not recurring work — it is a fan-out consumer of an event that already fired, and its extension point is deliberately open with a shipped precedent. Reason 3 is withdrawn and should not be cited again. D-001’s surviving reasons — a sink cannot backfill, and the apps’ contract is batch+cursor — are correct, and are exactly why the batch path is kept rather than replaced.

The item numbering in the first draft did not exist

The phase table listed P-001…P-009. The plan’s actual items are P-002…P-010 — there is no P-001. Two of D-006’s “effect on” pointers were off by one as a result. Corrected below and recorded as D-009; the reasoning was unaffected, only the pointers.

Design decisions

Where each call now stands

D-001 + D-006

Push for freshness, batch for completeness — both

Revised
Chose

A push sink on the event and a cursor-driven batch path. Steady-state freshness is ingestion latency; the batch path backfills and reconciles.

Was

A scheduled routine alone, accepting one interval of latency, with push deferred as a later optimization.

Why

“Instantly fresh” removes the latency trade, but a push sink still cannot backfill — it only ever sees future events — and cannot self-heal a delivery it dropped. Neither mechanism is correct alone.

D-008

The push half is an additional sink, not a new mechanism

New
Chose

An app-delivery sink registered through the existing additionalSinks extension point, modelled on personal-vault/live-sink.ts.

Rejected

A binding action that posts to an app.

Why

Extending an open extension point with a shipped precedent is reuse, not invention. A binding cannot do this: the action vocabulary is two types and one of them is hardcoded to a single app’s work-item kind. It must send a single-row batch through the same schema and endpoint the reconcile path uses — a push-only payload shape is how the two silently diverge.

D-002

The app owns its watermark; the producer asks

Load-bearing
Chose

Each run calls the app’s GET /api/sync/cursor and sends from there.

Rejected

A producer-side watermark table.

Why

A producer-side watermark silently desyncs whenever an app’s store is reset: the producer believes it delivered rows the app no longer has, and the app stays short with nothing failing loudly. Asking the receiver is self-healing.

Now also

This is what makes push and batch composable. Because the app owns the watermark and both apps upsert by id, the two paths deliver without coordinating: an overlap is an idempotent upsert, and a dropped push is caught by the next reconcile.

D-003 + D-007

Delete the app’s duplicate judgment door

Revised
Chose

Remove work-item minting from Email’s POST /api/triggers/gmail. The platform binding stays the single judgment path.

Was

Leave it alone and route content sync around it.

Why

Verified safe, and cheap. No platform caller of that route exists — the live judgment path is gmail-flagship → b7976842 → the binding engine, so the app’s endpoint is reachable only by hand. And the two halves are already separate calls: service.ts:135 runs ingestCanonicalMessage() and then seam.enqueueWorkItems(). Keep the first, drop the second.

Rule

An endpoint that both stores content and triggers judgment is not a sync endpoint. This now deletes the conflation instead of avoiding it, and restores the app to consuming a generic system rather than hosting its own trigger.

D-004

Read the canonical store, not the delivery log

Stands
Chose

personal_documents — canonical and deduped.

Rejected

trigger_deliveries.

Why

The delivery log holds one row per sink per event. It currently carries 2,359 email rows against 683 actual messages — and the vault sink alone holds 993, more rows than there are documents. Consuming it would deliver each message several times and make correctness depend on sink fan-out. For auditors: the “135 calendar-event rows” a prior grading cited as real-data evidence is a delivery-log count, not anything either app received.

D-005

Email gains a batch ingress mirroring Calendar’s

Stands
Chose

POST /api/sync on Email taking { messages[], contacts[], cursor?, sourceRef? }.

Rejected

Looping the singular endpoint once per message.

Why

No cursor means no watermark to resume from (breaking D-002) and no status to record. Two apps consuming one generic system should present the same ingress contract, or every future consumer special-cases them. It wraps the existing idempotent ingest rather than reimplementing it — which is also what makes the D-003 split cheap.

Eleven items, three phases

Phases are a real sequence, not decoration: the producer in Phase 2 cannot be written until Phase 1 settles the contract it posts into. Two items are new since the review — marked in green.

Status

Nothing is blocking. Two things worth knowing.

The corpus is live, so “done” is a moving target

Mail arrives continuously — the newest canonical row landed minutes before this page was written, while calendar has been quiet since 2026-08-28. Any acceptance evidence that cites a fixed row total will be stale before it is graded. The receiving-end assertion has to be “this provider id is in the app’s store,” never “the app holds N rows.”

Retracted: the “no single surface for every rule” opportunity

I claimed this page’s own “genuine opportunity” — that no surface lists every rule — and then measured it. It does not hold, and I withdraw it (D-010).

I said three stores; there are five, and one of them (event_reactions) is a fire log, not rules at all. Worse, the idea conflated two unlike things: trigger bindings (7) and ECA rules (26) are curated policy, while event awaits (790 live of 81,058) and predicate watches (387) are ephemeral runtime waits. A unified view would bury 33 rows of policy under ~1,177 rows of ephemera — worse than any surface it replaced. And each family already has a good one: events:graph gives on→fire edges plus a cause-chain for “why did this fire?”, /admin/triggers covers bindings, events:status covers live awaits. The code/data split is deliberate, not drift.

What survives is one missing edge, and it is premature. events:graph can’t see bindings; /admin/triggers can’t see what happens after an action fires. So “a Gmail message arrives — what happens?” spans four surfaces. Real, but not worth building against 2 armed bindings. Revisit when the binding population grows or a cross-plane misfire actually costs debugging time.
